Why scale-free out-degree helps a blind learner

A recurrent network learns by random-walking its weights until a scalar readout settles on target. Networks with heavy-tailed out-degree converge where Erdős–Rényi graphs almost never do. A single-hub model captures part of that advantage, and low dimensionality captures another part. Neither is enough on its own. The rest comes from where the leverage sits: a few incoming weights on broadcasting hubs, and hubs that drive other hubs.

The picture so far

Observability

A quiet readout

Hub-driven activity is low-dimensional, so the readout fluctuates little and can sit inside the tolerance window.

Necessary but not sufficient. Networks pinned to a fixed point are even lower-dimensional and fail.

Controllability

Leverage on hub inputs

An out-hub has three or four inputs and fans out to dozens of nodes. Each input weight moves a global variable.

The 9 input weights of the top three hubs recover about half the learning gain.

Hierarchy

Hubs driving hubs

Big broadcasters preferentially receive from other broadcasters, so leverage compounds along the chain.

Rewiring about 1% of edges, the hub-to-hub ones, costs 0.05–0.08 in convergence.

Starting point: out-degree decides it

The original sweep at N = 1500 crosses three degree distributions for in- and out-degree. Every ensemble with scale-free out-degree converges in about two thirds of trials or more. Scale-free in-degree with binomial out-degree converges in 2%. The notebook keys are ordered (out, in) while graph-tool's sampler returns (in, out), and that ordering is now checked, so the rows below really are out-degree.

Only out-hubs keep working as N grows

At fixed mean degree 3.5, Erdős–Rényi convergence falls to zero by N = 480. Scale-free out and a single clamped hub stay flat. Scale-free in holds to N = 240 and then drops, with its fraction of ordered trials falling from 0.68 to 0.35.

A single hub helps only on a ridge

The lumped hub as first written is alive in only 10–45% of trials, because its self-loop is drawn with probability α and needs weight above one. Forcing the self-loop gives a working hub. Its field quenches chaos more as σ grows, but convergence peaks at intermediate σ and collapses at σ = 40. There the network is ordered 90% of the time, yet the fixed point is pinned so hard that the random walk cannot move the readout.

Low dimension is necessary, not sufficient

Participation ratio measured before learning predicts which trials converge, and it keeps predicting within ordered and within chaotic trials. But convergence does not collapse onto it. Adding more clamped hubs drives the participation ratio to one and convergence falls back down. Pooled across all eleven conditions, convergence peaks at a participation ratio between about 1.2 and 2.

Leverage sits on the input edges of out-hubs

Restricting learning to subsets of weights separates structure from dimensionality. In scale-free out networks, the 9 input weights of the three biggest broadcasters recover about half of the full learning gain, and removing them from the learnable set costs convergence. The corresponding weights in Erdős–Rényi, whose biggest broadcaster reaches only about ten nodes, do nothing. In scale-free in networks the hubs are saturated sinks, so neither their 153 inputs nor their 8 outputs help.

This is also why the one-hub approximation undershoots. Its learnable weights are the hub's outputs, each moving one node, while the hub state itself stays fixed. The leverage in real scale-free networks is on the hubs' inputs.

Hubs driving hubs add a further share

In a configuration-model graph, a node's inputs are drawn in proportion to out-degree, so the largest broadcasters are fed by other broadcasters. Rewiring only the hub-to-hub edges among the top-k hubs, so each hub receives from a random bulk node instead, lowers convergence against both the intact graph and a placebo that rewires the same number of bulk-to-hub edges. That is about a fifth of the gap to Erdős–Rényi at 0.10, from roughly 1% of the edges. Without those edges scale-free out still converges at 0.27–0.29, so individual hub leverage carries most of the effect.

Smaller results

  • Learning barely moves the weights. Converged solutions sit about 10% of the weight norm from where they started. Growth of the hub-column norm matches the noise integral, 42 measured against 43 predicted.
  • A good fixed point often exists at initialization. With learning switched off, a clamped hub still converges in 10–18% of trials against 3–5% for Erdős–Rényi. Learning roughly doubles to triples that.
  • The hub column alone is a sufficient search space once its noise is scaled to its weight size. Learning only its 60 weights gives 0.38, the same as learning all 900.
  • Converged solutions are not marginal. Their leading Jacobian eigenvalue is about −0.70, more stable than non-converged endpoints, and about 70% survive a weight kick. Some stay weakly chaotic in directions the readout does not see.
  • At small N, sparsity is a confound. At N = 120 a sparse Erdős–Rényi graph with the same mean degree as the scale-free ensembles gets most of their advantage. The heavy tail separates only as N grows.

Open questions

Does the hierarchy share grow with N?

At N = 240 the hub chain is short. At N = 1500 hubs are larger and chains deeper, and Erdős–Rényi convergence is already zero.

TestRepeat the hub-to-hub rewiring with the JAX code at N = 1500.

Is per-weight leverage the right single quantity?

The sensitivity of the settled readout to each weight should be heavy-tailed in scale-free out networks and concentrated on hub and sub-hub input edges. Its size and spread might predict convergence across every ensemble, including the pinned ones where participation ratio fails.

TestCompute ∂y*/∂Wij at the fixed point and correlate with convergence per trial.

What is the smallest model with all three layers?

A sub-hub driving a hub that drives the bulk, with the hubs' input weights learnable. It should land between the one-hub model and full scale-free out.

TestTwo dynamic hubs with weaker σ, learning on input edges only.

Do in/out degree correlations matter?

If leverage scales like kout/√kin, then nodes with many outputs and few inputs are best. Anti-correlated in- and out-degree should help and correlated should hurt, at identical marginals.

TestDegree-preserving swaps that tune the in/out correlation of each node.

Can the ridge be predicted?

Convergence peaks between chaos and pinning. Static-input mean-field theory gives a chaos boundary in field strength and gain, and the ridge may sit at a fixed distance from it.

TestMap convergence over σ√α and g, overlay the Rajan, Abbott and Sompolinsky 2010 boundary.

Does scale-free in really decay at large N?

The drop at N = 480 rests on 60 trials. It is also the result closest to the original 0.02 for binomial out with scale-free in.

TestN = 480 to 1500 with 200 or more trials per point.
